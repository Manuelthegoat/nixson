Warning

If someone has already contacted you offering to get your money back

Be extremely careful. Approaching fraud victims with an unsolicited recovery offer is itself one of the most common follow-up scams. Lists of people who have already lost money are traded between criminal groups precisely because those people are the easiest to approach a second time.

What we handle

Different frauds leave different trails. What follows is what each type of case actually involves, and — just as importantly — what limits it runs into.

Bank transfers

Authorised push payment and invoice fraud

You were persuaded to send a transfer yourself: a fake bank "safe account" call, a hijacked email thread changing the payment details on a real invoice, a conveyancing or payroll redirection.

These cases move fast. Funds are usually pulled out of the receiving account within hours, so the first priority is a recall request through the sending bank and, where the scheme allows it, a reimbursement claim. Our work is establishing the account chain and documenting the deception clearly enough that the claim is not dismissed as an ordinary payment you simply regret.

  • Account-to-account chain reconstruction from your statements and the bank's response
  • Reimbursement claim and complaint drafting, including escalation to the ombudsman
  • Attribution of the beneficiary account holder and any company registered behind it

Cards

Card fraud and unauthorised charges

Unrecognised charges, subscriptions you never agreed to, or a merchant that took payment and delivered nothing. Card scheme chargeback rules give you a defined route with defined deadlines, and most failed claims fail on evidence and timing rather than on the merits.

Where a chargeback is the right route, we will tell you how to run it yourself for free. We take on these cases when the sum is significant, the merchant is disputing, or the transaction sits across several cards and accounts.

Digital assets

Cryptocurrency theft and fake trading platforms

Public blockchains are the one place where the trail is genuinely permanent. Transactions can be followed from your wallet through mixers, bridges and intermediary addresses to the point where funds reach a service that knows who its customer is.

That last point is the whole game. Tracing is straightforward; recovery depends on the funds landing at a regulated exchange that will respond to a properly evidenced request or a court order. Where they land at an unregulated platform in a jurisdiction that will not cooperate, a trace may still be useful for a police report or a tax loss claim — but we will be clear that the money is unlikely to come back.

On "crypto recovery experts"

No one can reverse a confirmed blockchain transaction, extract funds from a wallet without its keys, or "hack the scammer back". Anyone offering that is selling you a second fraud.

Investments

Investment and boiler-room fraud

Cloned firms using a real regulated company's details, unregistered brokers, forex and commodity schemes, and platforms showing a rising balance that cannot be withdrawn without paying one more charge.

The investigation covers the corporate structure behind the platform, the payment processors that handled your deposits, the domain and hosting history, and whether the entity was ever authorised in the jurisdiction it claimed. Processor-level evidence is often the most productive route, because processors are regulated even when the platform is not.

Social engineering

Romance and long-term relationship fraud

These cases usually involve many payments over many months, through several channels — transfers, gift cards, crypto, parcels. They are also the cases where people most often delay reporting, because the loss is not only financial.

We handle them the same way we handle any other: reconstruct the payment timeline, identify the channels that are still recoverable, and set out plainly which ones are not. We will also point you to specialist victim support, which is free and, in our experience, matters as much as the investigation.

How a case runs

Five stages. You approve each one before it starts, you know what it costs before it starts, and you can stop at any point.

  1. Assessment

    You send us the timeline and the documents you still have. We look at how the money left, where it went, how long ago, and whether any regulated party sits in the chain. At the end of this stage you get a written answer to one question: is there a realistic route worth pursuing?

    If the answer is no, this is where it stops. We will tell you what the remaining free options are and we will not sell you an investigation that cannot go anywhere.

    Typical duration: 3 working days · Cost: £350 fixed, credited against further work

  2. Engagement

    If there is a route, we set out the scope, the stages, the fee for each and what each one is expected to produce. Nothing proceeds until you have that in writing and have agreed to it. There is no open-ended hourly arrangement.

    Typical duration: 2 working days

  3. Tracing

    The investigative work. Depending on the case that means reconstructing the account chain from bank records, following transactions across public blockchains to a service that holds identity records, or tracing a platform back through its payment processors, corporate filings and domain history.

    You get progress updates at agreed points, including when the news is that a line of enquiry has closed.

    Typical duration: 3–6 weeks

  4. Evidence package

    A written report: the timeline, the payment chain, the attribution of each counterparty, the supporting exhibits with an index, and the methodology used to reach each conclusion. It is written to be handed straight to a bank fraud team, a regulator, a police unit or your solicitor without needing to be reworked.

    The report says what is established and what is inferred. Those are not the same thing and we do not blur them.

    Typical duration: 1–2 weeks

  5. Escalation

    Getting the package in front of whoever can act: a recall or freeze request to the receiving institution, a complaint to the regulator or ombudsman, a submission to the relevant law enforcement agency, or disclosure material for your solicitor to use in a civil claim or freezing application.

    Decisions at this stage belong to those bodies, not to us. What we control is that they receive a case that is complete and properly evidenced.

    Duration: depends entirely on the institutions involved

Honest limits

What determines whether anything comes back

Mostly things that were decided in the first few days, before anyone was hired.

How quickly it was reported

Recall and freeze requests work on hours and days. A case reported the same week has options that a case reported six months later does not.

Where the money landed

A regulated bank or exchange can be compelled or persuaded. An unregulated platform in a non-cooperating jurisdiction usually cannot.

How many hops it took

Each transfer through another account, wallet or bridge adds cost and reduces the chance of a recoverable endpoint. Some trails simply end.

Whether records survived

Deleted messaging threads, closed accounts and expired platform logs remove evidence permanently. This is why preserving everything matters so much.

A note on expectations

In a meaningful proportion of cases the honest outcome is a documented explanation of what happened and a report you can use for a police file, an insurance claim or a tax loss — not returned money. We would rather you know that before you engage us than after.

Report it yourself. It's free.

Everything on this page costs nothing and you do not need us to do any of it. If you only read one page on this site, read this one — and read it before you pay anybody, including us.

  1. Call your bank, then get it in writing

    Use the number printed on your card or your bank's official app — not a number given to you by whoever contacted you. Ask them to attempt a recall of the payment, to note the exact date and time you reported it, and to give you a reference number. Card and transfer disputes have deadlines, and the reported date is what those deadlines run from.

  2. Secure your accounts

    Change passwords for your email first, then banking, then everything that shares that password. Turn on two-factor authentication. If you installed any remote-access or "support" software during the incident, remove it and have the device checked before you log back into anything financial.

  3. File an official report

    Report to the police and to your country's fraud reporting body. This creates the official record that banks, regulators and insurers ask for, and it feeds the intelligence that gets networks shut down. The list below is a starting point — confirm the correct body for where you live.

  4. Write the timeline down now

    Dates, amounts, account numbers, wallet addresses, transaction IDs, phone numbers, usernames, website addresses, and how each contact was made. Do this while it is fresh. Screenshot conversations before accounts are deleted — they often are, within days.

Second-wave fraud

How to recognise a recovery scam

Being defrauded once makes you a target for being defrauded again. Contact details of victims circulate, and follow-up approaches offering to recover the lost money are among the most common frauds there are. Apply this list to us as rigorously as to anyone else.

  • They contacted you. Unsolicited calls, emails, WhatsApp or social messages offering recovery are the single strongest warning sign.
  • They guarantee results. No investigator, lawyer or agency can guarantee recovery. Nobody.
  • They want a fee to release your funds. A "tax", "clearance charge", "commission" or "deposit" needed before recovered money can be sent to you is always fraudulent.
  • They want payment in crypto or gift cards. Legitimate firms invoice and are paid through traceable channels.
  • They claim to be from a government agency. Agencies do not charge for fraud recovery and do not cold-call offering it.
  • They press you to decide today. Manufactured urgency is a tool for stopping you from checking.
  • They ask for remote access, passwords or seed phrases. No legitimate investigation ever needs these.
  • You cannot verify them. No registered company, no licence number, no verifiable address, no named people.

Before you engage any firm, including this one

Look the company up in the official companies register for its stated country. Check any licence number directly on the regulator's website rather than through a link the firm sent you. Ask for the fee structure in writing. Then wait a day before deciding — a legitimate firm will still be there tomorrow.

Support

The part nobody mentions

Fraud is not only a financial event. Shame, insomnia, and a reluctance to tell family are the normal response, not a personal failing — these schemes are built by people who do this professionally, and they work on careful, intelligent people every day.

Victim support services exist in most countries, they are free, and they are worth using. In the UK, Victim Support (victimsupport.org.uk) offers free, confidential help to anyone affected by crime, including fraud. In the US, the Identity Theft Resource Center (idtheftcenter.org) provides free one-to-one support. If you are struggling badly, please talk to your doctor or a local crisis line.
